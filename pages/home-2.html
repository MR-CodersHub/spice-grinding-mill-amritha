Teach a Chef, Not a Recipe

Anyone can hand you a masala formula. What a kitchen actually needs is someone who understands why the batch tastes different in October, why the cardamom went quiet, and what to do about it before the dinner service.

WHY THIS EXISTS

A Two-Day Course That Changes a Menu

We built this after watching the same problem repeat across a hundred kitchens: the dish is right, the spice is wrong, and nobody in the building can say which. The course takes a small group onto the mill floor, into the lab, and then back to a bench where they build a blend from raw components.

There are no slides. There is a granite chakki, a set of samples at four granule profiles, a moisture meter, and a cook who has to defend every component they put in the pan.

  • Maximum six per cohort. So everyone gets hands on the stones, not a view of them.
  • You bring a dish. We build the masala for it, and you leave with two kilos of what you made.
  • Nothing is recorded. No video, no notes taken by us. The recipes belong to the kitchens that make them.

THE POINT OF IT ALL

What You Leave With

  • Two kilos of the masala you built. Enough to cook with for a fortnight and to judge honestly.
  • A granule reference card. Four profiles, described by what they do to a dish rather than by microns.
  • A storage protocol. Container, temperature, volume and how long you have actually got.
  • A reorder route. The trade desk, the batch card and the one person who will remember your blend.

We do not run a certification. There is no badge at the end, because what you have learned belongs to your kitchen and not to us.

Bringing a team?

Groups of eight or more get a private cohort on dates we agree, and we can run it at your kitchen instead of ours for the practical half.
